The Case for Simple Tools

July 2026 · Engineering notes

Why the best engineering decisions tend to lead to the simplest implementations, and why complexity usually signals a failure of judgement rather than depth of skill.

Simple means maintainable

Simple means understandable, and understandable means maintainable. That sounds like a slogan, but it explains almost every maintenance problem I have run into: what makes code hard is not reading it, but reading through layers of structure that did not need to exist.

Every extra layer of abstraction is another place things can break. Abstraction is not free; the bill arrives as the amount of code you have to read the next time something goes wrong.

A decision rule that holds up

  • Anything you do three times deserves automating, but that does not mean it deserves a system.
  • Reach for the standard library and what the platform already gives you before pulling in a dependency.
  • When a simple approach solves the problem, do not add the feature you might need later.

Two traps that keep catching people

The first is designing for a scenario that has not happened. YAGNI keeps being repeated because it is counter-intuitive: leaving room for the future always looks prudent, but it stakes the requirements you can actually verify on guesses you have not.

The second is configuration sprawl. One switch per feature looks like a single line each, until the switches add up to a combination table nobody dares to touch, and the maintenance cost far exceeds the value of the features.

Where these conclusions come from

These judgements come from personal engineering experience, without systematic empirical backing. They hold for the projects I have worked on, and would need to be tested again elsewhere.
