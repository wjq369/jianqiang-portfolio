Wealth of Nations: Core Ideas

September 2026 · Reading notes

What the book gets right, what it leaves open, and why it still rewards a curious reader. This is the skeleton I kept while reading the full translation, not a chapter-by-chapter summary.

The plan of the book

The introduction already splits the whole book in two: labour productivity rises through the division of labour, and the share of productive labour rises through capital accumulation. The first two books take one each; the third and fourth explain why European policy ran against the natural order of opulence, and where mercantilism and physiocracy went wrong; the fifth turns to the revenue of the sovereign.

Division of labour

The greatest improvement in the productive powers of labour, and the greater skill, dexterity and judgement with which it is applied, seem to have been the effects of the division of labour. The pin factory is the example everyone quotes.

But the division of labour was not designed by anyone. It grew slowly out of the propensity to truck, barter and exchange one thing for another, and it is limited by the extent of the market — a market too small cannot support the split.

Price and distribution

Exchange needs money, and money begins as a way around the inconvenience of barter. Smith separates real price from nominal price, breaks price into wages, profit and rent, and then separates the natural price from the market price.

On wages he has a line that is often read backwards: it is not the actual greatness of national wealth, but its continual increase, that raises the wages of labour.

Capital, and the natural order

The second book turns to capital: the division of stock, money as one part of the general stock of society, accumulation, productive versus unproductive labour, stock lent at interest, and the different employments of capital. The number of productive labourers is proportional to the capital employed.

The third book gives the natural order of opulence: agriculture first, then manufactures, then foreign commerce. In Europe the order was in many respects reversed — the great improvement of agriculture came as a consequence of manufactures and foreign trade.

The invisible hand, once

The invisible hand appears exactly once in the whole book, in Book IV, chapter 2: in pursuing his own interest a man is led by an invisible hand to promote an end which was no part of his intention. Later readers have given it far more weight than the single paragraph it occupies.

Policy and taxes

The fourth book works through mercantilism chapter by chapter: wealth identified with gold and silver, restraints on imports, bounties, commercial treaties, colonial monopoly. The conclusion is that once all systems of preference or restraint are removed, the obvious and simple system of natural liberty establishes itself, leaving the sovereign three duties — defence against external violence, an exact administration of justice, and certain public works.

The fifth book covers the revenue of the sovereign, and its first maxim of taxation is contribution in proportion to ability: the equality or inequality of taxation depends on how far this principle is respected or neglected.

Still unverified

The author's dates and the year of first publication cannot be found in the text of this translation; they come from the prefatory matter of a different edition, which makes them second-hand. Nor does the translation state which English edition it follows. The reading behind these notes covers the introduction and key passages of each book, not every chapter.
