Einstein on Education

September 2026 · Reading notes

The line about education that gets quoted everywhere, and what the writings behind it actually say. The notes here cover a letter from 1953 and a passage from Einstein's essay On Science.

The letter, and the two pillars of science

In a letter of 23 April 1953, Einstein puts the two pillars of Western science plainly: the formal logical system invented by Greek philosophers in Euclidean geometry, and the discovery, in the Renaissance, that systematic experiment can find causal connections. That Chinese sages did not take these two steps is, in his words, no cause for astonishment — the astonishing thing is that they were taken at all.

Imagination, order, and religious feeling

  • Trust intuition and inspiration. Imagination is more important than knowledge, and it is a real factor in scientific research.
  • The belief that the world is in essence orderly and comprehensible is the foundation of all scientific work, and that belief rests on a religious feeling.
  • Science weakens superstition by developing logical thinking, and science exists for its own sake rather than for self-declaration.
  • Laws are never exact. We express them through concepts, concepts develop, and they will still prove insufficient.
  • Science and music differ in origin but share a goal — expressing the unknown — and their strongest shared motive is escape from the monotony of daily life into a world of created images.

The limits of the understanding

He uses two analogies, a globe and a clock, to make one point: most errors in philosophy and logic come from the human intellect treating symbols as if they were something real.

The bug analogy is the only complete chain of argument in the material: a two-dimensional creature can understand the third dimension mathematically but cannot intuit it, and a three-dimensional being stands in the same relation to the fourth. For God, a thousand years and a thousand dimensions are one.

What is not Einstein

The notes also contain a five-part contrast between desire and wish — essence, direction, relation to reason, moral colouring, metaphor — which has no direct connection to the scientific material and reads as an independent insertion by whoever organised the notes. It should not be quoted as Einstein's view.

About the famous line

Education is what remains after one has forgotten what one has learned in school is widely attributed to Einstein, but it falls outside the material these notes are drawn from. What is sourced here is the 1953 letter and the passage from On Science — and it is worth keeping the two apart when quoting.
